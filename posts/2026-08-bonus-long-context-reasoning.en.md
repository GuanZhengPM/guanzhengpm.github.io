## 1. [Taking It for Granted] Models Can Read More and More, and We Have Forgotten How Cool That Once Felt

In 2023, long context began to move from a model specification discussed in laboratories to a product capability that ordinary users could clearly feel. After Kimi launched, long documents became one of its most distinctive selling points. For many Chinese users, it was genuinely exciting to drop a PDF containing dozens or even hundreds of pages into a model and watch it read, summarize, and continue answering questions about the material. In the past, when we faced a book, a paper, or an industry report, we had to read it from beginning to end ourselves, or first search online to see whether someone else had written a summary. Suddenly, a model could read it for us and compress a long document into a few paragraphs within seconds or minutes. That alone was a striking experience.

Only a few years have passed, and we already take these capabilities for granted. Today, if a flagship model lacks a context window of hundreds of thousands or even millions of tokens, multimodal inputs such as images, video, and audio, or the ability to run tasks for a long time, users may feel it is not flagship enough. Kimi K3 now combines a 1M-token context window with visual understanding, long-horizon programming, and end-to-end knowledge work. In 2024, Gemini 1.5 was among the models that brought million-token, multimodal long context into the mainstream, and its technical report tested even longer ranges. Models can read more tokens, more types of tokens, and can keep running for longer periods of time.

Technology products often develop this way. When a capability first appears, it feels astonishing. After we use it for a while, it becomes something we assume should be there. When smartphones first introduced high refresh rates, fast charging, and computational photography, people discussed those features individually. Today, a flagship phone is expected to have them, and their absence is more likely to attract criticism. Only products that were clearly overpowered for their time, or that changed what users expected, continue to be mentioned again and again: the Xiaomi Mi 6, the GTX 1080 Ti, the iPhone X. A product becomes a classic because, at a particular moment, it redefined what a good product was supposed to be.

Kimi had a similar influence among Chinese users. It was not the first model in the world to provide long context, but it was one of the relatively early models in China to make long context a central selling point. It did not suddenly become the best model at every long-document task, either. But it allowed many ordinary users to feel, for the first time, that a model could read far more than they could. Long context used to be an idea found in papers, model specifications, and technical communities. Kimi turned it into a concrete product experience. Users did not need to understand attention mechanisms, KV Cache, or how tokens were counted. They only had to upload a file, ask a question, and feel the difference.

In 2024, OpenAI released o1-preview and gave "thinking longer before answering" a very clear product identity. After DeepSeek-R1 was released in 2025, the use of reinforcement learning to train reasoning capabilities became a much broader topic of discussion. These were not the first models capable of reasoning, but they were among the earlier models to turn the Reasoning Model into a product category that ordinary users could perceive and discuss on its own. OpenAI describes reasoning models as using internal reasoning tokens to plan, compare alternatives, handle ambiguity, use tools, and solve multi-step problems. The DeepSeek-R1 report discusses behaviors such as self-reflection, verification, and dynamic strategy adjustment emerging through reinforcement learning. People once thought models simply "predicted the next word." Later, models did not merely continue the sequence: they also broke problems down, tried different paths, and checked their results. We accepted that change very quickly too.

We are excited to use the newest and strongest models. On release day, we try them and compare them with the previous version. When the context window grows from 128K to 1M, when reasoning effort can be adjusted from low to high, and when a model can continue working for hours, it feels as though technology has taken another step forward. That excitement is reasonable. I also enjoy trying new models and new products. The question is: while models gain longer context and stronger reasoning, have the people using them changed in comparable ways?

We talk every day about whether models are robust enough and whether their reasoning chains are rigorous. But are we really more robust and more rigorous than the models? Maybe not.

## 2. [The Capacity Illusion] A Long Context Window Is Not the Same as Real Long-Context Capability

Even today, the industry may not have a single definition of "long-context capability" that everyone accepts. Compared with long-context capability, the engineering definition of a context window is clearer: it usually refers to the maximum total number of tokens a model can process in one inference. However, different models and APIs do not always count inputs, visible outputs, hidden reasoning tokens, and other content in exactly the same way. The size of a window and the model's ability to use what is inside that window are also two completely different things. A model labeled as supporting 1M tokens can accept that material, but this does not mean that at token 900,000 it still remembers a definition introduced at token 10,000. It certainly does not guarantee that the model can connect evidence scattered throughout the context and reach the correct conclusion.

This is similar to a person attending a three-hour meeting. The person sat in the room for three hours and heard every sound. Does that mean they understood the meeting? Of course not. They may have lost focus after twenty minutes. They may remember the opinion of the final speaker but forget the question raised at the beginning. They may even understand every sentence without understanding how those sentences relate to one another. What entered the ears was the "window capacity." What was actually understood and used was the capability.

Long-context research currently observes a model's ability to process long information through many different tasks. Some tests ask whether a model can retrieve relevant information from a long document or connect facts located far apart. Others examine whether it can count, aggregate, and summarize large amounts of information, or understand the overall subject, structure, relationships between people, and timeline. Some studies look at whether a model, late in the context, can still follow definitions and constraints introduced at the beginning; whether it can exclude irrelevant, conflicting, or deliberately misleading material; and how quickly performance declines as the context grows. Different studies divide these abilities in different ways. The abilities overlap, and it is difficult to capture all of them in a single complete and mutually exclusive taxonomy. As a non-exhaustive summary, they roughly include information retrieval, multi-hop tracing, aggregation and synthesis, global understanding, long-range consistency, resistance to interference, and robustness as length increases.

"Needle in a haystack" tests used to be very popular. A sentence was hidden inside a very long text, and the model was asked to find it. The test was intuitive and easy to communicate: either the model found the needle or it did not. But finding one sentence is only the most superficial form of long-context capability. The RULER paper argues that simple retrieval cannot represent full long-context understanding, so it adds tasks involving multiple needles, multi-hop tracing, and aggregation. Many models in the study claimed to support long windows, yet their performance still fell substantially as the input became longer and the task became more complex.

The statement "this model can read one million tokens" can therefore create a capacity illusion. It sounds as though the model has loaded all one million tokens into its brain and formed the kind of global understanding that a careful human reader might form. In reality, the material may simply have entered the range of computation. In real tasks, the position of information, its density, distracting material, and the form of the task can all affect the result. A model may accurately retrieve one clause from a contract but fail to judge conflicts among a dozen contracts. It may summarize every chapter while missing the central argument of the book. It may remember what the user said in the first turn but gradually drift away from the original goal during a long task.

All right, we have spent a long time discussing model evaluation. Let us return to people. When we evaluate someone's reading ability, we often stop at whether they have "read" something. How many books have they read? How many papers? How many podcast episodes have they heard? How many meetings have they attended? Quantity itself starts to look like capability. But having read something is not the same as finding the important information. Finding it is not the same as connecting it. Connecting it is not the same as understanding it. Understanding it is not the same as forming a judgment, and forming a judgment is not the same as using it in a new problem.

If we evaluated people using the same standards used for long-context models, many people might fail even the needle-in-a-haystack test. They read a report dozens of pages long but cannot locate the data supporting its central conclusion. They attend a long meeting but cannot explain what was ultimately decided. They work on a long-term project but, three months later, cannot explain why it was started. Multi-hop tracing, global understanding, resistance to interference, and long-range consistency would be even harder.

When model vendors release a new version, they tell us how much the context window has expanded and how much the benchmark scores have improved. A person's context window has no single number and no release notes, but that does not make it unimportant. Precisely because models can process more and more information, the human ability to understand what the model has processed is becoming a new bottleneck.

## 3. [Generation Surplus] Models Can Produce More Content Than Many People Can Consume

Obtaining an industry study used to be expensive. You had to find reports, search for data, and read papers. It might take days to form an incomplete judgment. Today, you can open Deep Research, enter a question, and let the model search, read, and organize citations automatically. Ten or twenty minutes later, you receive a report containing thousands or tens of thousands of words. It has an executive summary, a table of contents, tables, and references. Its structure is complete and its tone is confident.

Information is becoming cheaper, and the problem has shifted from "there is nothing to read" to "there is no way to finish reading it all." In the past, a person might carefully read one or two reports a week. Today, ten reports can be generated within minutes. The model's content production capacity has grown tenfold or a hundredfold, while human reading speed has not increased at the same rate. A model can search dozens of web pages in parallel. A person still has to read sentence by sentence. The model can summarize its own report again, but the summary is still text, and text still has to be understood by a human.

Recently, I gave more than twenty hours of recordings to a model and received nearly twenty thousand timestamped text segments. Speech-to-text can now be automated. What might once have taken a person many days can be processed by a machine in hours or tens of hours. But once the text had been generated, was the work finished? Of course not. Which recordings were valid and which were silent? Which people appeared repeatedly, and which voiceprint was probably mine? Had speaker labels been mixed up? Had the model generated nonexistent speech during silent passages? Which parts were worth organizing and which were just casual conversation? None of these questions answered themselves merely because twenty thousand pieces of text now existed.

The model solved the problem of "having no text" and created the problem of "having too much text." With twenty hours of recordings, the most immediate difficulty used to be that no one could finish listening. After transcription, the new difficulty is that no one can finish reading. Moving information from one medium to another does not mean understanding has taken place. The model performs the expansion; the human still has to perform the compression.

The same is true of Deep Research. Many people feel overwhelmed as soon as they receive the report, especially when it is full of unfamiliar concepts and jargon. They recognize every individual word, but do not understand what the words mean together. Every paragraph appears reasonable, yet after reading the report they cannot say which paragraph mattered most. In the end, they read only the opening summary and final recommendations. They do not inspect the evidence chain in the middle, and they do not open the cited sources. On the surface, they have used Deep Research. In practice, they have asked a model to generate a longer answer and then chosen to believe it.

Jargon makes the problem worse. In the past, when an article was difficult to understand, readers might suspect that the author was poor at explaining the subject. Today, a model can fluently use large numbers of technical terms and place several adjacent but different concepts in the same paragraph. The reader is more likely to suspect that they themselves lack knowledge. Agent, MCP, Skill, context engineering, reinforcement learning, model self-evolution: each term may be real, yet placing them together does not necessarily produce a meaningful argument. The more professional terms a text contains, the more it resembles a professional report, and the less an ordinary reader dares to question it. Eventually, "I did not understand it" becomes "it must be profound," rather than "perhaps it was not explained clearly."

Tutorials are changing in similar ways. A course used to be updated once every few years. Today, a model is released, a tutorial appears the next day, a "step-by-step beginner guide" appears on the third day, and a systematic course arrives within a week. Tutorial production is accelerating, and learners easily develop a sense of urgency: they have not finished learning one thing, and another has already appeared; they have just installed one framework, and the next framework is already more popular. They spend every day catching up and every day feeling behind. But installing a tool and running a demo are very far from understanding what problem the tool solves, why it was designed that way, and when it will fail.

Another pattern is even more common: asking the model to keep generating. The first response is not comprehensive enough, so the user asks for more. The expanded answer lacks examples, so the user asks for examples. A new term appears, so the user asks for a detailed explanation. The conversation grows longer, the amount of information grows larger, and the original question gradually disappears. The user receives more content but not a clearer judgment. The model works very hard, and the person feels they are learning, but together they have produced a pile of information that no one has truly digested.

We used to worry about filter bubbles, about platforms showing users only what they wanted to see. Generative AI creates another problem: each person can now produce an unlimited quantity of information tailored to their own preferences. If you believe a conclusion, the model can find ten reasons supporting it. If you like a particular style of expression, the model can continue persuading you in that style. Information has not decreased. It has become smoother, more complete, and more aligned with what you already expect. Without a habit of actively searching for counterexamples and verifying sources, a person may become even more trapped inside their own judgment than before.

More model output does not mean more human learning. Saving one hundred articles does not mean reading them. Reading does not mean understanding. Asking a model to summarize something does not mean the summary has become your knowledge. One of the great illusions of the AI era is that we confuse "receiving an answer" with "gaining a capability." The answer is in the chat window. The capability has not grown inside us.

## 4. [Information Loss] People Also Forget the Original Question in Long Articles, Long Meetings, and Long-Term Projects

Context loss in models is easy to notice. A requirement is made explicit in the first turn, and by the twentieth turn the model has forgotten it. A long document defines something early on, and when asked about a later section, the model cites the earlier definition incorrectly. An Agent is assigned a long task and gradually starts optimizing unimportant details, drifting away from the original objective. We say the model is unstable, that its context management is poor, or that it lacks long-range consistency.

People do the same thing. We simply do not usually call it context loss.

A meeting begins with the goal of solving a user problem. The discussion shifts to technical architecture, then to who will own the work, and finally everyone decides to schedule another meeting. Everyone speaks at length, the meeting ends on time, and the original problem remains unsolved. A project is approved because it is supposed to create value for users. During execution, the team keeps adding requirements, metrics, and presentation materials. Several months later, the team's main concern is launching on schedule rather than whether the thing being launched creates value. Someone begins a job search because they want a direction that suits them. Later, they spend every day editing their resume, comparing roles at different companies, and reading interview guides, until they forget why they wanted to change jobs in the first place.

These are all failures of human long-range consistency. As information, participants, and time increase, the original objective is gradually covered by new details. The newest problem always feels most urgent. The opinion of the final speaker is easiest to remember. The easiest metric to quantify is most likely to become the goal. People do not lack context; their context is continuously crowded out by noise.

Organizations lose context too, and often faster than individuals. Only the earliest members of a project may know why it was designed in a particular way. After several rounds of staff turnover, new members see only what the system looks like now. They do not know the constraints that existed at the time or which alternatives were abandoned. They either treat historical decisions as naturally correct, or they tear down every design that looks unreasonable and repeat the same mistakes their predecessors made. If documents record decisions but not their background, and meeting notes preserve conclusions but not evidence and disagreement, organizational context disappears when people leave.

Many forms of so-called "big company disease" may also be context problems. Information is repeatedly compressed as it moves between organizational levels. The execution layer sees a local task. Management sees an aggregated number. No one sees everything that happened in between. Each layer makes a reasonable judgment based on the portion it can see, and the combined result is unreasonable. The company does not lack smart people. It lacks any person or Agent with complete context, and it lacks a mechanism for reconnecting the information scattered throughout the organization.

Human multi-hop tracing is not as strong as we imagine either. To analyze a company, we need to connect its product, team, technology, finances, and industry cycle. To judge a person's capability, we need to distinguish what they participated in, what they truly owned, and how much of the outcome came from the platform and the team. To evaluate a role, we need to connect the job description, the interviewer's background, the stage of the business, the role's position in the organization, and its future potential. Each piece of information is not difficult on its own. The difficulty lies in putting them together without allowing one especially vivid detail to dominate the entire judgment.

Many bad decisions are not caused by missing information, but by using only local information. People choose companies based only on cash compensation, choose stocks based only on recent prices, evaluate candidates based only on school and employer names, and evaluate products based only on launch demos. Each local fact may be true, yet the global conclusion drawn from those facts can still be wrong. The longer the context, the easier it becomes to overlook the difference between a local optimum and a global optimum.

Resistance to interference matters just as much for people. A new model is released today and everyone says one direction is the future. Another company raises money tomorrow and everyone says a different direction is the future. WeChat Moments, Xiaohongshu, group chats, and the media manufacture new consensus every day. People are easily pushed along by every new wave of information. They study Prompt today, RAG tomorrow, Agent the day after, and model self-evolution two days later. They have encountered every term and gone deep on none of them.

This creates a powerful illusion: I encounter the newest information every day, so I must be growing quickly. In reality, the volume of input may have increased while the system used to make judgments has not changed. Being carried forward by the times and growing through your own effort are two different things. You can stand on a high-speed conveyor belt and move very quickly, but that does not mean you have learned how to run.

My definition of human long-context capability is not the ability to memorize a ten-thousand-word article or remember everything everyone said in a meeting. It is the ability, amid large amounts of detail, long spans of time, and high levels of noise, to remember the original question, identify what is relevant to it, connect scattered evidence, discover contradictions, and ultimately form your own judgment. Remember what should be remembered and forget what should be forgotten. The goal is not to preserve all context, but to manage context.

## 5. [Outsourcing Reasoning] A Model Thinking Longer for You Does Not Mean You Have Acquired Reasoning Ability

Long context concerns placing and using more information. Reasoning concerns how we move from information to a conclusion. The two abilities are related, but they are not the same. A model can have a very long context window yet do little more than search within it. A model can also perform strongly in mathematical and coding reasoning but struggle with dozens of conflicting real-world documents.

If a model thinks longer on a person's behalf, does that person become better at reasoning? I do not think so.

In the past, a person who could not reason well might have struggled to produce a complete analysis, and the holes in their argument would have been easier to see. Today, they can ask a model to generate a complete report with premises, arguments, examples, conclusions, and even citations. The output looks far more rigorous than before, but the rigor belongs to the generated text, not necessarily to the person using it. The user may not have checked a single source or have the ability to judge whether the intermediate inferences are valid. They accept the answer because it is long, well structured, and confident.

That is more dangerous than before. In the past, not knowing something usually looked like not knowing something. If a person could not express an argument, they were more likely to realize that they did not understand it. Today, the model can fill the gap in expression and make a person appear to have formed a view. Once the view has been generated, the person easily develops a sense of cognitive ownership: "this is what I wanted to say." They then treat the model's argument as their own argument. The model performed the reasoning; the person copied the conclusion.

Reasoning ability is not the ability to write "first, second, and finally." It is not the ability to stretch a simple opinion into five layers. Real reasoning includes asking which premises a conclusion depends on and whether those premises are true; whether the evidence actually supports the conclusion or merely confuses correlation with causation; whether important variables or contrary examples have been omitted; whether the conclusion still holds when conditions change; and what the worst outcome would be if someone acted on that judgment.

In interviews and conversations about career choices, I often encounter a very typical form of reasoning: a friend joined a company and later did very well, therefore the company is worth joining; a colleague left a large company for a startup and failed, therefore startups are too risky; a particular role has recently become popular, therefore I should move into it too. The facts in those statements may all be true, but many conditions are missing between another person's outcome and your own decision. When did that person join? What did they own? What capabilities did they have? What risks did they accept? What is similar or different between you and them? Without answering those questions, "learning from successful experience" is simply allowing another person's outcome to make your decision.

Real-world problems are more troublesome than mathematics problems. Mathematics problems usually have explicit conditions and answers. Code can be tested by running it. Many real-world decisions have no standard answer. When choosing a company, hiring someone, building a product, or investing, information is always incomplete, participants may be dishonest, and the environment keeps changing. A model can list possible factors, but assigning weight to each factor still requires judgment. It can predict several outcomes, but only you can decide which outcomes you can bear.

This is why I do not agree with claims that "in the future, people will only need to know how to ask questions." Asking questions matters, but it is nowhere near enough. People still need to judge whether the model's answer is credible, whether a tool call was correct, whether citations support the conclusion, and whether important information was omitted. If a person lacks domain knowledge, reasoning ability, and verification habits, a stronger model may simply generate a more persuasive wrong answer.

There is also an important boundary here. We cannot inspect every step taken by a model, and we do not need to fully verify every low-risk question. If we ask what to eat today, polish a paragraph, or generate temporary code, we can often use the result directly. But when a question involves a career choice, an investment, medical care, company strategy, or an important product decision, we cannot hand over responsibility merely because we used an excellent model. The model can perform the computation. The human still bears the consequences.

Using a reasoning model and possessing reasoning ability are two different things. The former means calling an external capability. The latter means knowing when that capability is reliable, when it should be doubted, and when you must personally work through the problem again.

## 6. [Co-evolution] AI May Not Eliminate Differences Between People; It May Move Those Differences into Reading, Reasoning, and Judgment

When AI first became widely available, many people expected it to level differences in ability. A person who could not code could generate code. A person who could not design could generate images. A person who could not write could generate an article. A person unfamiliar with an industry could ask Deep Research to investigate it quickly. These tools have genuinely lowered many barriers and given ordinary people their first opportunity to complete work that once required a professional team.

But lowering the barrier to use is not the same as equalizing the final result. Given the same Deep Research tool, one person defines the question, limits the scope, requests evidence and counterarguments, checks sources one by one, and finally forms an independent judgment. Another person enters a broad question, receives a report containing tens of thousands of words, reads only the summary, and forwards it to someone else. Given the same Coding Agent, one person decomposes tasks, supplies context, writes tests, reviews the diff, and handles failure recovery. Another person generates a pile of code, stops when it runs, and has no idea what changed or when it might break.

The stronger models become, the larger this difference may grow. A capable person can mobilize a greater external capability and amplify their own reading, judgment, and execution tenfold. A less capable person can also generate more content, but without the ability to judge that content, they merely amplify their confusion tenfold. In the past, the difference between two people might have been that one could write and the other could not. In the future, both can generate an article. The difference is who understands what the article says, where it is wrong, and whether it should be published.

This divergence will also appear at work. In the future, many roles will have access to similar models, and the baseline capabilities available to everyone will become more similar. What separates people may not be who heard about a tool first, but who can give the model more complete context, judge the quality of its results, and keep a long-running task from drifting. Once tools become universal, the tools themselves are no longer a moat. The user's ability to manage complex problems becomes the moat.

When we used to say that a senior person had experience, we often meant that they remembered a great deal of historical context: why something had been done in a particular way, which alternatives had already been tried, and which metrics looked impressive but created no value. AI can store this information, but it cannot automatically decide which parts of history should be reused now and which forms of path dependence should be abandoned. Truly excellent people can maintain long context without becoming trapped by history. They know what happened before and can still update their judgment when the environment changes.

Long-form reading will become important again. Not because everyone must return to the age of printed books, but because models will continue giving us longer and more complex results. Deep Research, codebase analysis, comparisons across many files, long-video summaries, and Agent execution traces all create large amounts of intermediate information. If someone lacks the patience to read long material and the ability to maintain context, the original document makes them feel faint, so they ask the model for another summary. If the summary is still difficult, they ask for a simpler explanation. The information becomes shorter, details and conditions disappear, and the final result consists of statements that are correct but useless.

Writing will matter too. I do not mean asking a model to write more beautifully. I mean whether a person can reorganize complex information themselves. Writing a long article requires maintaining definitions, the order of an argument, examples, and conclusions. By the end, the writer still has to remember what was said at the beginning. Writing itself is training for human long context and for reasoning. When you cannot express an idea clearly, the problem is often not expression. You may not have thought it through.

Long-form writing has another value: it forces contradictions into the open. An opinion written in a short social media post can easily appear correct. Expand it to several thousand words, and you have to explain where it applies, find examples, and address counterexamples. Many conclusions that once felt certain begin to wobble. When you reach the end and discover that something at the beginning was too absolute, you go back and revise it. That is a real reasoning process. A model can help find material and check structure, but if the model writes the entire article, the person loses this opportunity to expose contradictions and revise their own judgment.

How should people train these abilities? I do not think we need to invent a "human long-context boot camp." Many traditional methods still work. Read long texts carefully instead of reading only summaries. Decide what question you are trying to answer before you begin. After reading, close the original and write the conclusion in your own words. Separate the author's facts, inferences, and value judgments. When you encounter an argument you strongly agree with, actively search for a counterexample. During a long-term project, return regularly to the original objective and check whether the current work has drifted away from it.

Of course, people do not need to outperform machines in every dimension. There is no value in calculating faster than a calculator or memorizing every piece of material. Machines are good at storage, search, generation, and computation. Humans should spend more energy on goals, trade-offs, value, and responsibility. But this does not mean people can abandon reading and reasoning. Quite the opposite: the stronger the external capability becomes, the more the user needs to understand what they are doing.

Today, it is easy to feel that our own capabilities have been upgraded. We subscribe to the most expensive model, use the newest Agent, open the largest context window, and upload all our material. It feels as though we have upgraded alongside the tool. In reality, the tool was upgraded, not the person. A tool can change in one day. A person does not acquire a new cognitive structure by clicking an update button.

Models continue to expand their context, spend more computation on reasoning, learn to use tools, and run for longer periods. People also need to update their capabilities: maintain goals across longer information, find evidence among more opinions, remain skeptical in the presence of stronger generation, and resist being carried away by every new wave of information in a faster-moving era.

AI can read many things for us. It can think through many steps for us. It can generate an answer that looks excellent. But choosing what to believe, what to do, and which consequences to accept remains a human responsibility.

As model context grows longer, human context cannot become shorter. As model reasoning grows stronger, people cannot be left with nothing but copied conclusions.

Being carried forward by the times is not the same as growing. Using the strongest model is not the same as possessing the model's capabilities.
