## 1. [Think for Yourself] Early in your career, do not simply follow the advice of HR, recruiters, or people who graduated before 2023

At every new stage of life, keep your own point of view. Advice from parents, seniors, teachers, and HR can be treated as reference material. They may mean well, but good intentions can still lead you in the wrong direction—and you will bear the consequences. They may not be malicious; everyone simply has limits.

The habit of thinking for yourself should be developed in middle and high school. Should you move to another city for a better school? Board or commute? Choose a private school? Which subjects should you take under the new gaokao system? Which university choices matter most to you—school, major, city, or avoiding “wasted” points? Should you work after graduation or continue studying?

Without your own judgment, you end up saying things like: “My homeroom teacher told me to stay local,” “My parents would not let me study elsewhere,” “The admissions consultant said this school would not waste my score,” “My relative said this major has good job prospects,” “The first mock exam is closest to the real gaokao,” “The third mock was harder than the first,” or “My counselor said everyone in our major goes to graduate school.” These suggestions may sound reasonable, but they often recycle an old solution from a particular moment. Their shelf life is poor, and much of the information is hearsay with an extremely low signal-to-noise ratio.

I know that setbacks as a student—failing the high-school entrance exam, the gaokao, or graduate-school admissions—can be painful. I have experienced some of them too. I am not bringing them up to reopen wounds or refuse reconciliation with my past self. I bring them up because, at a new stage of life, we should ask whether we truly listened to ourselves when deciding. Was what you wanted something you had genuinely longed for, or something you picked up from a trend or rumor? Was the choice actually yours?

Now let us move from school to work. “HR said the role was product operations but that I would be doing product-manager work; the actual job was nothing like what we discussed.” “My manager advised me not to leave, and they have treated me well.” “The recruiter said someone in my situation would have trouble finding another job.” “I heard on Xiaohongshu that…” “A colleague said the length of internships matters more than the number of them.” “Nobody can find a job after 35.” It looks much like middle school: a torrent of noise and a stream of possibly useful, possibly useless information from peers and seniors.

An abundance of information pushes outcomes toward the average. It drags a high-upside choice back toward the center of the normal distribution. Experience always expires. Its useful life may be a second, an hour, a day, a week, a month, or a year—sometimes longer, sometimes shorter—but no experience is eternal. Retrospective judgments based on the past can sound persuasive even when their expected return has already fallen below the market over the long sweep of history.

We often hear, “Learn from people who got results.” I do not think that statement is reliable on its own. Getting one result does not automatically make someone exceptional. A better version is: “Learn from people who keep getting results across cycles.” Do not learn only from someone who succeeded during an upswing. The people worth trusting are those who can still produce major results in a downturn. Why across cycles? Because bull- and bear-market strategies can be opposites. In a rising economy or industry, almost any move can pay off; without understanding your temperament, the chain of reasoning behind your decisions, and the facts on the ground, you can still lose heavily in a bull market.

Back to the title: why do I advise people early in their careers not to blindly follow HR, recruiters, or seniors who graduated before 2023? Because most HR professionals, recruiters, and working people are not as expert or outstanding as we imagine; I will say more in the next section. Many HR staff, recruiters, and interviewers are not professional. Some job descriptions ask for three to five years of agent experience. Did their authors check how many years of agent experience even Shunyu Yao has? ReAct appeared on arXiv in October 2022. Even if we say Yao has four years of agent experience, how many people in the world have three to five? After Manus went viral in 2025, many companies began building agents. How many have truly understood and reproduced Manus, Claude Code, or Codex? How many call a workflow or a function call an “agent”? Most candidates therefore have only one or two years of relevant experience. Asking for three to five is absurd. Time moved at normal speed before 2023; since then it has accelerated. We do not yet know by how much. Perhaps one day in AI equals a year elsewhere; perhaps the unseen frontier is moving even faster. Most earlier experience has already lost its validity.

I have worked across AI and recruiting, and I often share job descriptions and referral links, so I have met many HR professionals and recruiters. Truly good ones are rare. Most perform keyword matching. Once, because my résumé did not mention Figma, an HR person called to ask whether I could draw prototypes and requested a prototype portfolio. At that point I may already have completed ten product internships, and I did not know how to answer. Perhaps HR was only relaying the hiring team's request; if so, the request reflected the interviewer's poor judgment, and the role was not worth more time.

A real job search should move you up a level. It should teach you something new and increase your value. If you are chosen only because your past experience matches and your current skills can completely cover the new work, what do you gain beyond cash and title? Have you created larger or genuinely new value for the industry? Probably not. You have only changed a small circular track into a larger one. Across ten or a hundred years, that choice is a tiny fluctuation, not even an outlier. How likely is it that HR or a recruiter will help you make a true career leap? And if someone offers you the chance, are you capable of making the jump?

In school, if every teacher you met was exceptional, following them might have worked. What about university? What about after graduation? By then, most of us have witnessed the diversity of the human species. Do you still dare to outsource your judgment? On this short yet long journey through life, every new stage requires us to keep pace with the world, find its rhythm, and decide in line with our own convictions. Everyone is different, and finding yourself is lifelong work. I hope you live as yourself—and live brilliantly.

## 2. [Look Inward] Exceptional people are always a minority; in most companies and teams, most people are ordinary, and perhaps half are not very good

I mentioned HR and recruiters above not to attack them as a group, but because I see many young candidates blindly accepting their unreasonable judgments. That can genuinely mislead people.

A capable recruiter friend once told me that recruiters “do not tell the whole truth, but do not lie.” He is professional and tries to communicate information to candidates and clients rationally and objectively. Few people manage even that. Yet withholding a truth that materially harms a candidate is, in substance, a kind of lie. If a company is unstable, why not help candidates prepare mentally? If a candidate has a crucial weakness, why not tell the client? Is everyone afraid the recruiting fee will disappear?

In daily work, we collaborate with product, engineering, algorithms, operations, and growth. I have spent time at more than twenty companies of different sizes. Even after working in core teams at major tech firms, among China’s “six AI tigers,” and at so-called star startups, the colleagues who truly impressed me—excellent collaborators whose judgments were usually right and who could admit their limitations—have been extremely rare. I often wondered whether I was too demanding of myself and others, too idealistic about work, or simply overawed before the spell broke. After discussing it for a long time with some of the strongest people I know, I reached a conclusion: too few people treat their work as a mission. Most are “startup enthusiasts,” not entrepreneurs.

Look at offer discussions on Xiaohongshu and similar forums. Most focus on pay increases, team atmosphere, and overtime. Few ask whether the job fits them, could help them build something of their own later, could become a decisive career springboard, or involves frontier work that is genuinely scarce. After joining, people often care only about their assigned slice. They may see bad cases in another vertical or from a teammate and act as though it has nothing to do with them—as if offering advice means stealing someone else’s job. I do not know which company or era made this normal. Perhaps it is a survival rule in large firms: mind only your own business. I have spent less time at big companies, so I cannot attribute it with confidence. I only know that it is wrong and suboptimal both locally and globally. When everyone treats what they are building as a job—not a cause and not a sincere attempt to create value—the atmosphere becomes suffocating. On an old business with little growth, where even the share price tied to one’s compensation has halved, it is understandable that people turn their attention elsewhere and merely go through the motions. But innovative teams full of people who claim to have an “entrepreneurial spirit” are often not much better. Work gets rougher and more impatient. People use slogans such as rapid experimentation and first principles to defend bad judgment rather than genuinely thinking and creating. Many can talk fluently about startups, fundraising, and new trends, yet cannot build an innovative project and would never dare start a company. They are not innovators or entrepreneurs; they are startup enthusiasts. We need to identify and support real entrepreneurs. Work and mission are different: a job can proceed step by step and allow work-life balance; entrepreneurship is a mission, and a mission demands full commitment.

Good product managers, engineers, algorithm researchers, direct managers, founders, and investors are all rare. We should remove the aura around a collaborator’s identity, résumé, and education, then speak with them rationally, objectively, and as equals. Learn from people who are truly excellent rather than merely look excellent; use people who look excellent but are not as mirrors for self-reflection. Do not accept a view simply because its owner stands higher, speaks louder, or has more power over your “future.” When you meet the virtuous, seek to equal them; when you meet the unvirtuous, examine yourself.

You should also ask what you want from a project or company. If the goal is to learn something unavailable elsewhere, lower pay or an imperfect atmosphere may be tolerable because you deliberately give more weight to learning. If several negative signals appear together—a stagnant business, suffocating culture, weak colleagues, and compelling opportunities outside—it may be time for the next stop. This is not a call to resign without a plan. It is a call to assess your current job and the real market objectively, then make the higher-return decision.

## 3. [Pursue Substance] Distinguish experience from ability; more experience does not mean stronger ability

Consider a freshman arriving at university. Older students can explain how to choose courses, where the library is, and which teachers grade generously. That is experience or simple knowledge—things you will probably learn with time anyway.

What is ability? It is something you may do better than everyone else at the school. If you place first in the ACM lab selection contest, that demonstrates coding ability; you may already outperform every senior who merely has more experience. Winning the campus singing competition or breaking a university athletics record also demonstrates ability.

My definition is that ability is a strength—or the combination of all your strengths. In other words, ability is an individual’s top skill or portfolio of skills. Passing China’s CET-4 or CET-6 English test does not by itself mean you are highly capable; it may carry only 0.1% weight in your portfolio. If your score is nearly perfect, however, English may be one of your top skills. Its weight depends on your other skills: it might represent 99% of your edge or only 1%. Everyone is different.

Once we distinguish experience and ability, we can discuss strengths and weaknesses. I have long believed that I should repair weaknesses before amplifying strengths. In the final round of my latest job search, an interviewer asked why. I described my first days at university: I failed every interview for student government and other organizations. Looking back, I had wandered off topic and had not taken them seriously enough. Still, the failures were discouraging. My idols were Elon Musk, Steve Jobs, and Lei Jun. I wanted even one percent of their achievements, and the distance between that ambition and my ordinary self felt enormous. What advantages did I have then? I read an average amount in middle school but fell in love with reading after the gaokao. That summer I read books about LeBron James’s business empire and survival at Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Zhihu essays on personal development in college, and posts about teaching yourself to code and becoming an engineer. I also liked asking exceptional seniors and peers for advice. Reading and seeking advice were my only supposed strengths. Were they useful for becoming exceptional? Yes, but not enough. Amplifying them at that moment would not have created much value. First I needed to close weaknesses and become more of a generalist. Then I needed to throw myself into concrete projects, be tested by real work, and discover or sharpen new strengths.

What does this have to do with everyday work? A great deal. When interviewing candidates, most hiring managers want someone with “solid fundamentals” and “a particular capability.” Solid fundamentals mean no major problem in school, previous employers, performance, or promotion history. The particular capability is the real reason to make an offer, and it is usually substantive—except in fields such as marketing, sales, or investing, where personal resources and soft skills may carry more weight. For an AI product manager, general capabilities might include relevant product experience, perspectives on both models and applications, and the habit of gathering industry information through multiple channels. Distinctive strengths might include a very large coding-token budget, a highly starred open-source project, or a long-running habit of reading papers.

A strength should be hard and demonstrable. Otherwise it may be an interest, not an edge. Listening to AI podcasts is an interest; anyone can listen, so what separates you from a high-school student? If you form your own ideas, act on them, practice, and achieve a result—perhaps producing an episode or emailing a guest you admire and securing a collaboration—then you have evidence of a skill or strength.

Experience easily creates the illusion that we are impressive. The same illusion appears when we assume an impressive school makes us impressive, a booming major makes us impressive, or a startup in a vast market makes our own work impressive. Reality is not so convenient. If we stay absorbed in work that never lands, we easily deceive ourselves—sometimes with help from the outside world, sometimes entirely on our own. Distinguish experience from ability, environment from self, collective achievement from individual contribution. Cut through the fog, keep sharpening yourself, get to the essence, and build genuinely hard-core capabilities.

## 4. [Create Value] Take non-consensus positions within major trends, and do difficult, valuable work—not different work merely for the sake of being different

What is a major trend? Foundation models, embodied intelligence, controlled nuclear fusion, and commercial spaceflight are major trends; we should move with them. More than a decade ago, mobile internet and globalization may have been the biggest trends, and work should have clustered around them. Today, the model itself is the application. Working close to the model is entirely reasonable. If your application layer creates no new value, moving nearer the model aligns with the larger trend and is nothing to be ashamed of. Do not build applications for the sake of building applications.

Why non-consensus? Consensus fields are already brutally competitive. You can choose to fight there, especially if you have ample resources and strong fundamentals; you cannot simply abandon the main battlefield. But what if your position is weaker—should you still enter the same melee? A non-consensus choice does not mean a small thing. It should be important and likely to become scarce, yet temporarily overlooked. Position early so that, at the right moment, you may earn an outsized return.

Why difficult? If the work is too thin, models will absorb it immediately—PowerPoint is an example. Easy work struggles to attract exceptional candidates or investor support. If something is small and simple, you can save some money and do it yourself; there is no need to assemble many people and much capital. Whether you build alone or join an organization, difficult work is more resilient. Something simple and obvious may not contain much value in the first place.

Why valuable? Some things are difficult but, in their actual context, useless. Others are unusual and look fancy, yet amount to ornament, empty form, and a great deal of thunder with little rain. Being idiosyncratic for its own sake is often stubbornness and fragility—a refusal to compete on the main battlefield. That differs from a non-consensus bet, which may become consensus later because it positioned early on the field that matters. A stubborn, valueless idea may help someone brag or sell a story, but it will not become consensus at another moment and will not become useful.

Do not pursue obscure, valueless “moats.” The work may be hard and the effort real, but if its return on investment is extremely low, abandoning it early is the responsible choice. As students, many of us heard about fixed patterns of thinking, the empty-cup mindset, and path dependence. You may have accumulated much experience and invested enormous effort, but the environmental variables and the formula have changed. Learning the new is important; forgetting the old can be even more important. Forgetting does not negate who you were. At that point in time, the old decision may have been your best option and your proudest achievement. Let the past remain past. This is a new beginning. Creating value is the objective; reusing an old feature or lowering the implementation cost of something new is not.

## 5. [Meet the Wave] In a fast-changing industry, embrace uncertainty and dare to choose

I have recently become fascinated by stocks. Nothing below is investment advice. I do not encourage beginners to trade blindly, and I do not encourage leveraged or debt-funded trading. Markets involve risk; invest carefully. I use investing only as a way to think about uncertainty, choice, reward and punishment, and under- or overvaluation.

Job choices, like stocks, require early positioning. It is now quite difficult to join four of the companies known as China’s “Six AI Tigers,” or the teams behind core AI products at major tech companies. Those teams also struggle to hire because their standards are high, yet much of the upside has already been captured. Why not join in 2023? If the new world was unclear then, why not in 2024—or 2025?

In 2024 and 2025, I recommended four of the “Six AI Tigers” through many different channels. Candidates, colleagues, and friends turned them down because the companies were disorganized, big firms seemed more prestigious, cash increases were below expectation, startup options looked like waste paper, some functions had low talent density, or certain leaders managed badly. To me, these problems were acceptable. On a fast-moving train that continues to accelerate, many issues can be temporarily ignored. As long as they do not stop the train or its acceleration, perhaps 99% can wait. Large companies are not necessarily better; *Trapped Inside DingTalk* is a classic example.

So what should we do when everyone seems flawed and every organization looks makeshift? Refuse to choose, stand still, and reject uncertainty? Of course not. Offense is the best defense. Choose the new and the frontier; advance with the industry, even if the work does not look like conventional “product” work or match older experience. Data and evaluation, for example, were treated as dirty work from 2023 to 2025, perhaps because search, ads, and recommendation teams developed a habit of looking down on bad-case review. In model work, however, data and evaluation are profoundly important. A central goal of an AI product manager is to help the model deliver stable, high-quality results. Work aimed at that essential source of value is not dirty work. The return gap between going somewhere new to do work that violates the last era’s expectations and going somewhere old to repeat familiar work can be immense—not 10% or 20%, but two, ten, or a hundred times.

Before 2023, people often said a top product-management offer was roughly equivalent to an ordinary engineering offer. In most contexts that was true. Algorithms were not valued in internet companies as they are today; they were simply one function alongside product, engineering, and operations, and most compensation was not extraordinary. I do not advocate a hierarchy between roles. I have always supported equality across functions and breaking down the boundaries between them. We should respect talent without encouraging privilege. Today the picture is different: the ceiling for algorithm talent may be ten, a hundred, or a thousand times the ceiling for product roles. The two no longer share an order of magnitude. Most things around us change linearly, so we rarely encounter exponential movement. Stocks are one place where we do. Again, this is not a recommendation to trade, use leverage, or borrow; markets involve risk. If the company you join grows tenfold in valuation in its second year, do you still care as much about cash? If it goes public in three years and grows a hundredfold between your start date and the listing? If it becomes China’s OpenAI, Apple, or Coca-Cola? Compare the share prices of Alibaba, Tencent, Meituan, Kuaishou, and JD.com. The opportunity at an established giant—or the title it offers—may no longer be decisive.

This does not mean startups are always good or big companies always bad. The first company where I interned has essentially collapsed. Every startup founder learns to speak like Jack Ma or another celebrity entrepreneur—about IPOs, market potential, missions, and values. We should encourage innovation and entrepreneurship, but we must also admit that 99% of startups are bad companies. Many fail across every dimension: talent density, quality of output, and return on the work they choose to do.

Companies can be under- or overvalued in both private and public markets; employees can too. Being overvalued is not necessarily good, while temporary undervaluation is normal. Trust that the market can eventually take you toward an appropriate price. Stay composed when submerged and calm when afloat; watch the rise and fall with perspective. When choosing stocks or companies, pay more attention to people who make predictions and less to those who only explain the past. Befriend people who dare to forecast: prediction is ten times harder than review.

Choose companies that are temporarily undervalued but valuable over the long run: buy where nobody is interested, sell where the crowd is deafening. As with stocks, joining an undervalued company is what creates the possibility of a large return. Following the crowd into an already overvalued company or stock invites market punishment. Sometimes joining a severely overvalued company can still make sense if you move quickly and use it to add a valuable line or title to your résumé. Stocks and offers are not identical, however. Holding cash can be sensible when markets are turbulent; standing still is not sensible when an industry is transforming rapidly. Before the wave of an era, if you do not advance, you retreat.

## 6. [Downside Thinking] If you can accept the worst plausible outcome, act

If you already have a safe fallback, do you end the current challenge—or make it harder?

If you hold only bad cards, do you play conservatively and wait, or play aggressively?

If the consequences of taking a risk would not truly destroy you, would you take it?

These are variations of the same idea: downside thinking. My interpretation is simple. If the worst outcome is merely uncomfortable, or if I could stand up again quickly after it happens, then I can accept the risk—and I will act.

If you fail the graduate-school entrance exam, can you accept trying again or working after your bachelor’s degree? Many people cannot. They numb themselves with “I will succeed on the first try” and avoid imagining the worst case, as if discussing it were unlucky. That is dangerous, especially for opportunities that come only once a year or once every few years. A choice that looks conservative may carry enormous risk.

If a stock you buy falls 50%, can you accept it? Warren Buffett has said that investors should be prepared for a decline of 50% or more and able to handle that volatility. If, after a 50% fall, you retain conviction, can act calmly while others despair, and can gradually work your way out, then the difference between that loss and a 10% loss is mainly the time required to absorb it.

If you join a startup to do challenging work and the company collapses six months later, can you accept searching for a job again? What if it collapses on your first day? If even that outcome is acceptable, listen to yourself and join.

Nothing is entirely risk-free. Bank deposits offer low returns, but banks can fail. Everyone has a different capacity for risk and a different temperament, so weigh risk and return for yourself. The world keeps getting harder, and doing the same things that worked before is yielding steadily lower returns. In an era of both opportunity and challenge, we may need to assume more risk simply to earn what we once did. How far to turn up that risk dial depends on the downside you can truly bear.
