## Preface

We have all heard a few sayings:

- A frog in gradually heated water does not notice it is being boiled.
- Be a friend of time.
- People are products of their environment.
- Gold will shine wherever it is.

The industry changes too fast. It is hard to make a complete judgment from the small sample of people around us. Even if I write a summary now, it may be out of date when I look at it again six months from now.

I think I started helping people share JDs and refer candidates around 2022. Most of them were friends looking for someone to take over their internship roles, or people recruiting for teams I was in. In 2022–2023 I probably only looked at one or two hundred résumés.

In 2024 I referred more candidates and started looking for résumés on Boss Zhipin and Xiaohongshu. I remember getting some kind of activity badge on Boss Zhipin. I spent a lot of time browsing the profiles there; across 2024 I probably looked at more than a thousand online résumés.

In 2025, I joined a startup doing AI plus recruiting. If nothing particularly urgent came up in the morning, I would open the company's talent database and browse résumés. I have never counted precisely, but by now I should have seen more than ten thousand. Mostly product, operations, growth, engineering, and algorithm candidates, the common roles in product and R&D; I have also seen some candidates in investment, HR, strategy, and design.

## On Résumés

A properly formatted résumé ≠ a good résumé ≠ a strong person ≠ a match for the role.

Using a fairly minimal template and writing a résumé properly is as basic as not turning up to an in-person interview disheveled or wearing slippers. But the return on this is very limited. If you do not have experience in xx, prettier formatting or a better display of your xx qualities cannot change that fact.

A good résumé usually means the person's past record looks decent and has no glaring weakness. But a good-looking résumé and a good-looking history are still not enough. If the interview shows that the person only knows how to package themselves, that is not very OK either.

As for role fit, it is more about whether you have a feel for the direction of the work. You may be very strong at A1, while the interviewer comes from A2 and the job itself is A2. They may not be able to see what was good about your A1 experience. It is hard to impress them, even if you are strong at A1 and A1 may even be harder than A2. You can still fail the interview.

And passing an interview, receiving an offer, and performing well after joining are a whole chain of separate questions. A candidate with a Nice résumé and a Nice background is still a very long way from bringing positive returns to the team after joining.

You also have to consider the candidate's own circumstances: whether they are married, have children, are willing to travel, are willing to move cities, and so on.

There are individual cases too. Someone you worked with very well, someone with an excellent reputation in the team, goes to interview at another company and fails in the first round.

## Personal Growth

Looking back at some résumés, some candidates rose very quickly; some chose the right company at a critical moment; some make you feel it is a pity; some seem to have grown “old”; and some have not lost a step.

The keywords in value investing are: good business, good price, understand it, hold on to it.

The keywords for a good colleague are: drive, ability, perspective, and foresight.

- Start with drive. Some colleagues have this limp feeling about them. They do not care much about many things and have little opinion or fighting spirit. (By drive I do not mean being unable to communicate, yelling at people, acting arrogant, or being hard to work with.) But business, products, and models all involve competition. Even if you do nothing, someone may come and take the ground you already hold. Without drive it is hard to keep what you built, for a company or a person.
- Ability: even if someone has no particularly impressive wins in their past, or much directly relevant experience, you can still tell who has real ability. My observation is that in most companies the proportion is roughly 1 in 30. In a company of 300, the genuinely strong people may fit on the fingers of one hand.
- Perspective means not letting a personal grudge or some small matter override the bigger picture and the group's interests. There is no point obsessing over something minor or secretly competing with a particular person. If everyone can make the thing bigger together, getting a little more or a little less yourself does not matter much. If nothing gets built, even 100% of the result is not worth much. A colleague with perspective wants to get the thing done together, and wants to do bigger things together.
- Foresight: when you see one company already declining and another beginning to take off, you should be able to make the right decision. It is the same within a project. When growth has slowed and the ceiling is in sight, more resources should go to work with room to grow. Trying to rescue a declining product or project can consume a lot of effort and money for very little.

When we call a colleague an “old-timer” or say they do not have much ability, the person probably lacks drive, ability, perspective, and foresight. They have lost their edge, do not really understand the work they are doing now, fight over meaningless resources and power all day, and all their supposed experiments fail. But they were not always like this. People once described them as very close to the front line, aggressive, professional, and attentive to what was happening at the frontier…

If people have families and are getting older, and their energy and thinking are starting to decline, you can understand it. Everyone gets old.

But then you find some interns and new graduates are like this too. You thought they were pretty good when you first met them; after some time you find that they have also grown “old.”

I find this very confusing. They are supposed to be on the way up. Why do some people keep getting stronger, one level after another, while others become ordinary?

We accept that, as time passes, our sprint speed and the number of pull-ups we can do may fall. But it is much harder to accept, or even notice, that we may be getting weaker in other ways. Some people seem as good as ever, even better; with others you feel their best days are behind them. Time rewards people who keep iterating and punishes those who stay the same.

Looking at people around me, and at all those résumés, I think there are roughly five reasons: ❶ the work really does not suit them, ❷ they give up too early at a critical moment, ❸ the organization's culture or their direct manager is the problem, ❹ they have no one better to learn from, and ❺ they get caught up in comparing themselves with other people.

- Wrong fit: they have not put themselves in the right place. At first, novelty and some of the qualities of a “good student” help them get results for a while. After some time, they cannot keep pushing. But the work looks glamorous, so they do not want to give it up, and do not want to be themselves. They end up stuck, conflicted, and wearing themselves down.
- Giving up too early: people need to be trained by real work. On some important things, you have to grit your teeth and get through the low point; the next time you face something similar it becomes much easier. Climb the mountain, and once you are over it the other mountains look small. If you do not make it over, you have no idea when you will get another chance to climb. Being on a key project at a key moment gives you rare and valuable experience and growth. Sometimes you really should push yourself.
- Organizational culture: some cultures are genuinely toxic. Sometimes it really is not your fault; the company, department, team, or manager is the problem. There is a reason some organizations keep getting results, and a reason others never do. Staying in a toxic organization is tiring and brings little return. Sometimes the tiredness itself is not even the big problem. Failing to build anything, or seeing good people passed over, is worse. Of course you cannot place all the blame on others. You have to reflect as well. If you are not recognized or promoted anywhere, think about what may be your own problem.
- No one better to learn from: when everyone around you seems about the same and you do not have many ideas of your own, it may be time to pay attention. The industry really does move fast, and there really are new things under the sun.
- The comparison trap: xx joins a big tech company, so you do too. xx has a certain number of internships, so you need more. xx gets into a 211 university, so you need a 985. Step out of the contest you are quietly having with one person or a few people, and out of the contest to show off among your peers. Many of these things can be ignored. Some are like unlocking a shared bike with a crooked handlebar: there is really not much to agonize over. A big tech company is not better than every other company at everything, and so-called entrepreneurship is no reason to look down on people who work for someone else. If you pay too much attention to these things, your own growth rate can fall behind the market without you even noticing. Letting things that barely matter take up your attention is a loss.

## Climb the Mountain

I have talked about people growing “old.” How do you avoid it? I do not really have an answer. Some people know a bit about every new direction and can quickly talk about every new term in the industry. But when you ask what they themselves have done, there is not much there. Watching the frontier matters; only watching it is not enough.

For example, if you want to enter a new industry, you can talk to recruiters, investors, tech media, and people through other channels, get enough information, then come back and think. Forcing a switch, taking a gap, or doing a master's or second master's may not be the most direct way to solve the problem. Sometimes the issue is simply that you do not have enough information. You have not even worked out who is doing things in this industry now or what kind of people they need, and you are already considering another degree.

But after reading and asking around, you still have to do the work yourself. You say a direction is good. Then you get into a project and some of the work is dirty and tiring, with no visible result in the short term. Do you still do it? Some things go smoothly at first, and at the first thing you do not know how to do, you want to change company or change direction. You may run into the same problem again. That is different from seeing that a project has no value and deciding not to waste more time on it. It is hard to tell the difference; maybe you only know after doing it.

Find what you love doing and take responsibility for yourself. Pick fewer low-hanging fruits, maybe pick a few, and keep moving toward a bigger and more distant goal. I think many people actually know what they want to do, but that thing is too hard or too slow, and nobody is necessarily going to offer them a job for it. Something easier is right in front of them, so they take it. They keep taking those things and eventually stop talking about what they originally wanted. Of course you have to eat and earn money. I am not saying you should reject every opportunity in front of you. You just cannot use that as the reason every time.

Being reliable, being someone others are willing to trust, matters a lot. When people have left a company and actually talk about former colleagues, the most reliable ones are not necessarily those with the highest title or salary. But if you were reliable and good to work with, people really remember. When a good opportunity comes up, they are willing to come talk to you. Do not treat a relationship or a project as a one-time deal.
